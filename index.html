What is Animedoro?

What is Animedoro?

Work for 40 to 60 minutes, then watch one anime episode (about 20 minutes) as your break. Repeat.

It is the longer-block cousin of the Pomodoro technique. Instead of 25 minutes on and 5 off, you get a real stretch of deep work and a break that ends on its own when the episode does. The name comes from a January 2021 video by Josh Chen, a medical student who used it to log around 600 hours of study and 300 hours of anime in four months. He calls it an extended 52/17 variant, and he originally ran it with sitcoms. The full Animedoro guide on my blog goes deeper into the origin, the ratio and how to stop at one episode.

Pomodoro's short blocks are great for getting started on something you are avoiding. Animedoro's longer blocks suit problem sets, reading and coding, where 25 minutes ends just as you get into it. Pick the work length that matches how long you can actually hold focus, and keep the break at one episode.

One episode, not four

  • One episode, then stop. Set the anime minutes to the real episode length and let the notification pull you back.
  • Skip the opening and ending. That is how a 24 minute episode fits a 20 minute break.
  • Pick a show you have already seen, or a slow one. Cliffhanger shows are how the second episode happens.
  • Not an anime person? Any 20 minute sitcom works. Josh Chen started with sitcoms.
  • ADHD note. Some people find the longer block easier than Pomodoro because focus has time to kick in, and a concrete reward is easier to commit to. The stop rule matters even more here.

Animedoro FAQ

What is the Animedoro technique?

Animedoro is a study method where you work for 40 to 60 minutes, then watch one anime episode, about 20 minutes, as your break, and repeat. It is a longer-block variant of the Pomodoro technique, named by medical student Josh Chen in a January 2021 YouTube video.

Animedoro vs Pomodoro: what is the difference?

Pomodoro is 25 minutes of work and a 5 minute break, with a longer break every four rounds. Animedoro is 40 to 60 minutes of work followed by a 20 minute break, and the break has a fixed shape: one episode. The longer work block suits deep tasks, and the episode makes the break something you look forward to instead of something you drift through.

Why 40 and 20 instead of 25 and 5?

Twenty minutes is the length of a typical anime episode without the opening and ending, so the break ends on its own. Forty to sixty minutes is long enough to get into a hard problem; many people find 25 minutes ends just as they are getting going. Josh Chen describes it as an extended 52/17 variant.

How long is an Animedoro session?

One round is roughly an hour: 40 to 60 minutes of work plus one 20 minute episode. Three to four rounds make a solid study session. There is no mandatory long break; the episode is the break.

Does Animedoro actually work?

It works when the reward is strong enough to get you to sit down and the stop rule holds: one episode, then back to work. The evidence is anecdotal, including Josh Chen's own 600 study hours in four months. The timer helps with the stop rule by notifying you when the episode time is up.

Is Animedoro good for ADHD?

Some people with ADHD prefer it to Pomodoro because a 25 minute block can end before focus has kicked in, and a concrete, time-boxed reward is easier to commit to than a vague five minute break. The risk is the second episode, so set the anime minutes to one episode and let the timer stop you.

Can I use the Animedoro timer with shows that are not anime?

Yes. Josh Chen originally used sitcoms. Any show with 20 to 25 minute episodes works, and you can set the anime minutes to whatever your episode length is.

Is there an Animedoro app?

This timer works on any phone or computer in the browser and can be added to your home screen like an app. It is free, open source on GitHub, needs no account, and keeps your session counts in your browser's local storage. It has been online since January 2021.
